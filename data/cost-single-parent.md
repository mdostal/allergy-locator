# The True Monthly Nut — Single Parent + One Toddler, One Income

**Scenario modeled:** one working parent, one toddler (age ~2–3) in **full-time
center-based childcare**, a single earned income, and **no meaningful federal tax
relief** (see the tax note below — at the income level the user is targeting, EITC
is gone and the childcare credit is a rounding error). Data is 2025–2026, verified
for the four decision cities. This is a *cash-flow* model (what leaves the bank
each month as a renter), not a net-worth model.

> **Headline honesty check:** Austin metro rents really *have* fallen — down ~21%
> from the summer-2022 peak (Zillow ZORI all-units ~$1,531, and the user's instinct
> that rents dropped is correct). **But a true 3-bedroom still runs ~$2,300–2,500**
> (3BR apartment avg $2,417, RentCafe Jul 2026). The user's remembered **~$1,800 is
> a 1–2BR or a below-market/far-suburb 3BR**, not the market 3BR.
>
> **CHILDCARE CORRECTION (this was wrong before):** an earlier pass called ~$2,000
> a "premium choice" and claimed a ~$1,200–1,450 "standard Austin floor." That number
> is a **metro/state blend** — it averages in cheap far suburbs (Round Rock ~$1,200),
> rural East Texas (Tyler ~$750, Whitewright ~$500), and low-cost slots that are **not
> realistically available** (UT Child Development Center, Extend-A-Care YMCA, church
> co-ops — all UT-affiliated, part-day, or 3–9 months waitlisted). **The user lived
> and worked in CENTRAL Austin and needed a central, actually-available toddler seat.**
> Every branded center that was genuinely open to enroll — **Primrose (~$1,991 for a
> 2-yr-old), Bright Horizons (~$2,000 unsubsidized, and cheaper *only* when
> work-subsidized), Stepping Stone (Mueller), Guidepost Montessori, Lil Miss
> Sunshine's (~$1,800 near work)** — clustered at **~$1,800–2,200**. The ~$2,000 he
> paid was **not a premium splurge; it was the effective central-Austin floor for a
> seat he could actually get.**

## The key question: was anything both cheaper AND available in central Austin?

**No.** In core/central Austin (Mueller, downtown, East Austin, near-UT, SoCo/
South-Central, near-work tech corridors), there was **no toddler center that was both
meaningfully cheaper than ~$1,800 AND had a short/no waitlist.** The two categories
never overlapped:

- **Cheaper on paper, but not available:** UT Child Development Center (~$1,400–1,600,
  but UT-affiliation + long waitlist), Extend-A-Care YMCA (sliding scale, but mostly
  school-age / part-day, not full-day toddler), church and co-op programs (cheap,
  waitlisted, or a few half-days). **Bright Horizons is only affordable when an
  employer subsidizes it — and even then it was 6+ months out; unsubsidized it's ~$2,000,
  on par with everyone else.**
- **Available to walk in and enroll, but ~$1,800–2,200:** Primrose, Stepping Stone,
  Guidepost, the neighborhood Montessoris, Lil Miss Sunshine's.

So the honest answer to "where should he have looked for cheaper-and-available?" is
**there wasn't one.** The only real levers were (a) an employer childcare subsidy he
didn't have, or (b) leaving central Austin for a suburb/rural market — i.e., the move.

## The tax layer (why "no meaningful federal breaks" is right)

At the **higher income the user is targeting**, the federal safety net has already
phased out — so it does **not** rescue the Austin (or any) monthly nut:

- **EITC:** max for one child is **$4,328**, but it **fully phases out around $49,084**
  (single/head-of-household, 2025). At a professional income → **$0**.
- **Child & Dependent Care Credit (CDCTC):** floors at **20% of up to $3,000** =
  **$600/yr max** above ~$43k AGI. Against ~$23k/yr of childcare that offsets ~2.6% —
  **a rounding error (~$50/mo)**.
- **Child Tax Credit (CTC):** the one real break — **$2,200/child** (~$183/mo), full
  until $200k. But it's **federal and identical in every city**, so it **cancels out of
  the relative comparison** entirely.
- **State income tax — the one that *does* move the comparison:** **Texas and South
  Dakota have none. Nebraska does** (~4–5% effective, ~$340–375/mo on a ~$100k income).
  **Moving Austin → Omaha *adds* an income tax that Austin never charged**, clawing back
  part of the renter's COL savings. Rapid City (SD) keeps the no-income-tax status Texas had.

**Tax-adjusted Austin-central → Omaha real savings ≈ $1,329 − ~$360 = ~$969/mo** (~$11.6k/yr).

## Austin home-price trajectory (2022 → 2026): was he paying a lot for a depreciating asset?

**Yes — his instinct was correct.** Austin home values **peaked ~May 2022 (~$550k metro
median) and then fell ~24%**, with the metro median now in the **~$426k–445k** range
(Zillow ZHVI / Redfin, still down ~3% YoY into early-mid 2026). Prices declined hard
through 2023–2024 and have since **plateaued** (analyst consensus: flat to low-single-digit,
not a rebound). Meanwhile **carrying costs stayed high** — 6–7% mortgage rates, Texas
property tax ~1.8–2.2%, and rising insurance — so a 2022 buyer was, for roughly two years,
**paying peak-era carrying cost on an asset that was flat-to-declining.** That is exactly
the "a lot of money for a depreciating asset" feeling, and the data backs it.

## Verdict — does leaving Austin actually save money?

**Yes — and by *more* than the earlier (wrong) analysis claimed, because central-Austin
childcare had no cheaper-and-available option. The ~$2,000 was the floor, not a splurge.**

1. **Renter, like-for-like:** Austin central ($6,632/mo) vs Omaha ($5,303/mo) is a
   **~$1,329/mo gap; Nebraska income tax eats ~$360**, leaving **~$969/mo of real
   savings (~$11.6k/yr)**. That is meaningful money, not the marginal ~$370/mo the prior
   pass estimated — the earlier number was too low precisely because it assumed a fictional
   $1,300 Austin daycare floor that didn't exist for a central, available seat.

2. **The pain was NOT a self-selected premium tier.** The correction reverses the old
   conclusion: **central Austin's *available* toddler floor was ~$1,800–2,000**, so the
   childcare cost was **geography, not a lifestyle choice.** The only way to pay less was
   an employer subsidy he didn't have — or to leave. Mueller adds a **housing** premium
   (+$768/mo vs central Austin) — that part *is* a walkability/lifestyle choice — but the
   childcare bill would have been ~$1,900 anywhere in core Austin.

3. **Buying makes leaving even more decisive.** On top of the renter's ~$969/mo, the
   **home-price gap** compounds: Austin ~$510k metro (city ~$567k, Mueller ~$740k+) vs
   **Omaha ~$299k** — a **~$210k+** difference (~$1,100–1,300/mo in mortgage/interest/tax)
   — *and* Austin values fell ~24% from 2022 while Omaha held. As a buyer, leaving Austin
   is a strong financial win; as a renter, it's now a clear ~$969/mo win.

4. **Rapid City** has the **lowest monthly nut ($4,978)** and **no state income tax**, but
   **homes cost more than Omaha** ($366k vs $299k) and **childcare supply is thinnest** — a
   buy-in and availability penalty that matters given the Black Hills / Wyoming endgame.

**Bottom line:** The cost case for leaving Austin **holds and is stronger than previously
stated: ~$969/mo for a renter (after NE tax), before the six-figure home-price gap.** The
honest correction to the earlier premise: **central-Austin childcare was not an optional
premium — ~$1,800–2,000 was the effective floor for a seat he could actually get**, the
Tyler→Whitewright→Round Rock→central-Austin gradient the user described is real, and Austin
home values genuinely fell ~24% from their 2022 peak while carrying costs stayed high.
